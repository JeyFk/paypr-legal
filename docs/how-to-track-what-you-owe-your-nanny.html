How to track nanny hours, payments, and what you owe

By Paypr · Updated September 18, 2026 · US households

Keep a weekly record of work and a separate record of payments. Start with any unpaid balance, add the pay due for the week, then subtract payments already made. If you use payroll, reconcile against its net-pay statement before deciding what to transfer.

For a simple balance with no payroll deductions: previous balance + new earnings − payments = amount still owed. Keep reimbursements separate so they do not get confused with wages.

A worked example: a short week and two payments

Suppose your agreed rate is $20 per hour, your nanny works five six-hour shifts, and $50 from an earlier week is still unpaid. These are example amounts, not recommended rates. This arithmetic example excludes payroll deductions, overtime, reimbursements, and other adjustments.

The September 21 payment settles the earlier week. Keep its actual payment date and the workweek it covers. Do not change the shift dates to make a payment fit.

What to record each week

  1. Define the workweek. Write down when the seven-day period starts. Keep each workweek separate, even if you pay every two weeks.
  2. Record actual shifts. Include the date, start and finish, and any break that can properly be excluded. Use separate rows for split shifts. Thirty minutes is 0.5 hours, not 0.30.
  3. Check the pay calculation. Keep the agreed rate, ordinary earnings, and any overtime or other pay adjustment identifiable. Log paid leave and guaranteed-hours payments separately from time actually worked. See the overtime guide for the questions to check.
  4. Record payments separately. For each payment, keep the date, amount, method, period covered, and a receipt or reference. Two payments need two rows, even when they cover the same week.
  5. Reconcile together. Check hours and corrections before payday. Match the payment record to the bank or payment-service receipt, then carry any unpaid balance forward.

These are practical tracking fields, not a complete payroll or tax record. Employers may need additional information and records under federal, state, and local rules. Keep sensitive identification and tax documents in an appropriate secure system, separate from a routinely shared timesheet.

Gross pay, net pay, and the balance in your tracker

Gross pay is earnings before payroll deductions. Net pay is the amount payable to the worker after those deductions. Employer cost also includes employer taxes and any other employment costs.

If a payroll statement shows $600 gross, $60 in deductions, and $540 net, a $540 transfer can settle that paycheck. Subtracting only $540 from a tracker based on $600 gross would leave $60 showing as unpaid. This is an illustrative reconciliation example, not a withholding calculation.

Use your payroll statement to establish the actual amount payable. Paypr does not calculate deductions or reconcile payroll withholding automatically. Do not mark an unexplained difference as paid; resolve it against the payroll record.
