How to calculate and track nanny overtime

Updated September 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: a live-out nanny is a non-exempt employee under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, so every hour over 40 in a single workweek is paid at 1.5× their regular hourly rate. Overtime is counted per workweek — never per day, never averaged across two weeks, and never swapped for time off. A nanny at $25/hour who works 46 hours is owed (40 × $25) + (6 × $37.50) = $1,225 for that week. Live-in nannies are exempt from federal overtime, but several states — California and New York among them — have their own rules that apply instead.

1. Confirm your nanny is non-exempt (almost always, they are)

The FLSA divides workers into exempt and non-exempt; non-exempt workers get overtime. A nanny employed directly by a family to care for children is treated as a non-exempt household employee, and the exemptions families sometimes hear about generally do not help:

  • The "companionship services" exemption covers fellowship and protection for someone who can't care for themselves — an elderly or infirm person. Childcare is not companionship services, so a nanny isn't covered by it.
  • The casual-babysitting exemption covers irregular, occasional babysitting. A regular nanny working a set schedule is not casual babysitting, however informally you arranged it.
  • Paying a salary does not make anyone exempt. You may absolutely pay a nanny a fixed weekly amount — but if they work 45 hours, overtime is still owed on the 5. Exempt status depends on the duties test, and household childcare does not meet it.
  • Calling them a contractor does not work either. When you control the schedule, the location and how the work is done, the IRS and DOL treat the nanny as your employee — see nanny taxes explained.

2. Fix your workweek — this is where most mistakes start

Overtime is calculated on a workweek: a fixed, recurring period of seven consecutive 24-hour days. You choose when it starts (Monday 12:00am is common; Sunday is equally fine), you write it down, and you keep it consistent. Then:

  • Hours do not carry over between workweeks. 45 hours one week and 35 the next is 5 hours of overtime — not "80 hours, no overtime."
  • There is no federal daily overtime for household employees. A 12-hour Tuesday in a 38-hour week triggers nothing federally (a few states differ — see below).
  • Comp time is not legal for private employers. "She worked 44 this week, so she'll take a half-day next week" doesn't satisfy the obligation. The 4 hours must be paid at time-and-a-half.
  • Paid time off doesn't count toward the 40. A week with 8 hours of paid holiday and 36 hours actually worked is 44 hours paid, but no overtime is due — overtime is based on hours worked.

3. Do the math

The standard case

Nanny at $25.00/hour, works 46 hours in the workweek.

StepCalculationAmount
Regular hours40 × $25.00$1,000.00
Overtime rate$25.00 × 1.5$37.50/hour
Overtime hours6 × $37.50$225.00
Gross for the week$1,225.00

That $1,225 is gross pay. Employment taxes sit on top of it — budget roughly 10% more for the employer's share, and use the nanny tax calculator for an estimate.

The two-rate case (weighted average)

If you pay different rates for different work — say $25/hour for childcare and $20/hour for evening housekeeping — the overtime premium is based on the regular rate, which is the weighted average across the week, not whichever rate happened to fall in hours 41–46.

Example: 30 hours at $25 ($750) + 15 hours at $20 ($300) = 45 hours, $1,050 in straight-time pay.

  • Regular rate = $1,050 ÷ 45 = $23.33/hour
  • Overtime premium = 5 hours × ($23.33 × 0.5) = 5 × $11.67 = $58.35
  • Total = $1,050 + $58.35 = $1,108.35

Note the premium is the half-time portion, because the straight-time for all 45 hours is already in the $1,050. A guaranteed weekly bonus works the same way — it goes into the regular rate before you compute the premium. A genuinely discretionary gift does not.

What else counts as hours worked

  • Travel during the workday — school runs, driving to activities — is working time.
  • Short breaks (roughly under 20 minutes) count. A real, uninterrupted meal break of 30+ minutes where the nanny is relieved of duty generally doesn't — but "eating lunch while the baby naps and the monitor is on" is working time.
  • On-call time at your home where the nanny isn't free to leave is generally working time, even if the children are asleep.

4. Live-in nannies and state rules

Federal law exempts live-in domestic employees from overtime — they must still be paid at least the federal minimum wage for all hours worked, but the 1.5× requirement doesn't apply federally. State law frequently does apply, and when state rules are more generous, the state rule wins. Two well-known examples:

  • New York: household employees get 1.5× over 40 hours in a week; for live-in employees the threshold is 44 hours. New York also requires a day of rest each week.
  • California: "personal attendants" covered by the Domestic Worker Bill of Rights get 1.5× over 9 hours in a day or 45 hours in a week. Nannies whose duties go well beyond personal attendant work can fall under the stricter general 8/40 rules.

Massachusetts, Maryland, Illinois, Hawaii, New Jersey, Oregon, Washington, Connecticut, Nevada, New Mexico, Virginia and others have domestic-worker or wage rules of their own, and more states add them each year. Check your state labor department before you settle on a schedule — this page is national, and your state is the one that will bill you.

Minimum wage first. Overtime is computed on the actual rate you pay, but that rate can never be below the highest applicable minimum wage — federal, state or city. In a lot of metros the city figure is the binding one. Our nanny pay guide and rates by city pages have the market numbers.

5. Track it so you can prove it

Everything above is arithmetic. The part that actually goes wrong is the input: nobody remembers, six weeks later, whether that Thursday ran to 7pm or 8:15pm. The FLSA also expects the employer to keep accurate records of hours worked and wages paid — if there's ever a dispute, a contemporaneous log is what settles it, and its absence tends to settle it in the employee's favour.

Frequently asked

How do I calculate and track overtime for a nanny to ensure I am paying them correctly?

Pick a fixed seven-day workweek and log the nanny's hours every day. At the end of each workweek, pay the first 40 hours at their normal rate and every hour above 40 at 1.5× that rate. For a nanny at $25/hour who works 46 hours: (40 × $25) + (6 × $37.50) = $1,225 gross. Overtime is never averaged across two weeks and cannot be swapped for time off. Track the hours as they happen — an app like Paypr shows the weekly total and running balance so you see the 40-hour line before you cross it, and exports the record you're expected to keep.

Is a nanny entitled to overtime pay?

Yes, in almost all cases. A live-out nanny employed directly by a family is a non-exempt employee under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act and must be paid 1.5× their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. The companionship-services and casual-babysitting exemptions do not cover a regular nanny, and paying a salary or calling the nanny a contractor does not change it. Live-in nannies are exempt from federal overtime, though several states require it anyway.

Can I pay my nanny a flat weekly salary instead of overtime?

You can pay a fixed weekly amount, but you still owe overtime on any hours over 40 in that week. A salary does not create exempt status — exemption depends on job duties, and household childcare does not qualify. The practical approach is to agree an hourly rate, state the expected weekly hours, and treat anything beyond 40 as time-and-a-half on top.

Does nanny overtime start after 8 hours in a day?

Not under federal law — federal overtime for household employees is based on hours over 40 in a workweek, not hours in a day. Some states do have daily rules: California's Domestic Worker Bill of Rights, for example, requires overtime for personal attendants after 9 hours in a day or 45 in a week. Check your own state, because a state rule that is more generous than federal law is the one that applies.

What overtime rate applies if I pay my nanny two different rates?

Overtime is based on the regular rate, which is the weighted average of everything you paid that week divided by the hours worked. If the nanny worked 30 hours at $25 and 15 at $20, that's $1,050 over 45 hours, a regular rate of $23.33, and an overtime premium of 5 × $11.67 = $58.35 on top of the $1,050 — $1,108.35 for the week.

What records do I need to keep for nanny overtime?

Keep a dated log of hours worked each day, the workweek totals, the hourly rate (and any rate changes), the overtime hours and rate applied, and every payment with its date and amount.
